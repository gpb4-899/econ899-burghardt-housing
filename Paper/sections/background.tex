\section{Mortgage markets in the euro area}
\label{sec:background}

When the ECB raises rates, the speed at which the increase reaches a
household's budget depends on a contract signed years earlier. In Finland,
Spain or Portugal the interest rate on a typical mortgage is tied to the
Euribor and resets within a year, so a policy change shows up in monthly
payments almost immediately. A German or French household, by contrast,
usually pays a rate that was fixed for ten years or more, sometimes for the
whole life of the loan, and notices a tightening only upon moving or
refinancing.

These differences are not accidents of the business cycle but stable
features of national housing finance. \citet{badarinza2018} document that
the share of adjustable rate mortgages in new lending varies enormously
across countries and persists over time. The roots lie in funding models
and regulation. Banks in Germany refinance mortgages with long term covered
bonds, which makes long fixation periods cheap to offer, while banks in
much of the periphery price their loans off short term interbank rates.
National histories of inflation and different prepayment rules push in the
same direction. For my purpose the important point is that a country's
position in this spectrum changed little over the sample period, which is
what allows me to treat mortgage market structure as a fixed country
characteristic.

House prices, meanwhile, followed very different national paths under the
common policy. Ireland and Spain went through a dramatic boom and bust
around 2008, German prices stagnated for a decade and then rose strongly
after 2010, and Greece saw a long decline during the sovereign debt
crisis. Such heterogeneity under a single monetary policy is exactly the
variation my design exploits, and it raises the question whether the
structure of mortgage markets is one of its sources.
