\section{Mechanism}
\label{sec:mechanism}

Why should mortgage structure shape how house prices respond to monetary
policy? The argument runs through household budgets and is often called the
budget or cash flow channel. A surprise tightening raises short term market
rates within days. In a variable rate country this feeds into the payments
of existing borrowers within months, so disposable income falls for a large
group of households at once, and with it both their demand for housing and
the size of the loans they can service. Sellers face buyers with tighter
budgets, and prices should give way. In a fixed rate country the same
tightening leaves most existing borrowers untouched. Only new borrowers and
refinancing households face the higher rates, so housing demand weakens by
less and prices should fall by less.

Other channels operate in both groups of countries and therefore create no
differential prediction on their own. Higher rates raise the user cost of
housing and reduce the present value of future housing services, which
depresses prices everywhere. Expectations of future policy work in the same
direction.

One further effect deserves mention. In fixed rate countries a tightening
can lock existing owners into their current loans, because moving would
mean giving up a cheap rate. Fewer homes come on the market, and the
reduced supply supports prices in exactly the countries where the budget
channel is weak. This lock in effect therefore reinforces rather than
offsets the main prediction.

Taken together, the mechanism implies that house prices in variable rate
countries should fall by more after a surprise tightening. This is the
prediction $\delta_h < 0$ tested in Section \ref{sec:strategy}.
