\section{Data}
\label{sec:data}

I build a quarterly country by quarter panel of eleven euro area countries,
Austria, Belgium, Germany, Spain, Finland, France, Greece, Ireland, Italy, the
Netherlands and Portugal, starting in 1999, the first year of the common
monetary policy. This section describes the four building blocks of the panel:
house prices, the monetary policy shock, the macroeconomic controls, and the
measure of mortgage market structure. Table \ref{tab:summary} reports summary
statistics for the estimation sample.

\subsection{House prices}

The outcome variable is the real residential property price index from the OECD
Analytical House Prices dataset, which I download through DBnomics. The series
is quarterly and is expressed as an index with base year 2015. Index levels are
not informative across countries, so I take logs and work with cumulative log
changes, which gives the house price response in percent and does not depend on
the choice of base year.

\subsection{Monetary policy shock}

The monetary policy shock comes from the Euro Area Monetary Policy event study
Database (EA-MPD) of \citet{altavilla2019}. The database records high frequency
changes in asset prices in a narrow window around each ECB monetary policy
event. I use the Monetary Event Window, which spans both the policy decision
and the press conference, and take the surprise in the three month overnight
index swap (OIS) rate as the interest rate signal. The three month rate is a
standard near term policy indicator and is considerably less noisy than the one
month rate.

A well known problem is that not every interest rate surprise is a pure
monetary policy shock. When the central bank reveals private information about
the economy, interest rates and stock prices move in the same direction, which
contaminates the surprise. I isolate pure monetary policy shocks using the
sign restriction of \citet{jarocinski2020}. For each event I compare the sign of
the interest rate surprise with the sign of the EURO STOXX 50 return in the same
window. When the two move in opposite directions the event is a pure monetary
policy shock and I keep the interest rate surprise. When they move in the same
direction the event is treated as a central bank information shock and set to
zero. I then aggregate the event level pure shocks to the quarterly frequency by
summation. Without this correction the raw surprise produces a counterintuitive
positive house price response to tightening, which the information cleaned
series removes.

\subsection{Macroeconomic controls}

I control for the domestic macroeconomic environment using three series from
Eurostat. Real gross domestic product (chain linked volumes, seasonally
adjusted) enters as the quarter on quarter growth rate. Consumer prices enter as
the year on year rate of change of the Harmonised Index of Consumer Prices.
Unemployment is the harmonised unemployment rate; I take the monthly series and
average it to quarterly frequency, since the monthly release reaches back to the
late 1990s and covers the whole sample, whereas the quarterly release starts
only around 2009 for most countries. All controls enter lagged one quarter so
that they are predetermined with respect to the shock. I also include a dummy
for the pandemic quarters (2020 through the first half of 2021), because quarter
on quarter GDP growth reaches roughly twenty percent in absolute value in those
quarters and would otherwise dominate the estimation.

\subsection{Mortgage market structure}

The moderator of interest is the structure of the national mortgage market,
specifically whether mortgages are predominantly fixed or variable rate. I
classify each country as adjustable rate dominant or fixed rate dominant
following \citet{badarinza2018} and related euro area evidence. Germany,
Austria, France, Belgium and the Netherlands are classified as fixed rate
dominant, and Finland, Ireland, Portugal, Spain, Italy and Greece as variable
rate dominant. This binary classification is transparent but coarse, since the
variable rate share in fact varies continuously across countries and over time;
I return to this limitation in the concluding section.
